\subsection{Research Motivation and Challenges}
Various industrial and agricultural applications require gas detection systems
that are low-cost, mass-producible, and power-efficient. Depending on the use
case, gas sensing may involve coarse detection for safety monitoring, accurate
measurement of chemical composition, or continuous concentration tracking for
process control. One critically important application domain is personnel
safety: many hazardous gases are imperceptible to human senses yet toxic at
trace concentrations. Gases such as $\mathrm{CO}$, $\mathrm{NO}_2$, and
$\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{S}$ are poisonous even at \text{ppm}-level
concentrations, while flammable gases such as $\mathrm{CH}_4$ and
$\mathrm{H}_2$, and asphyxiants such as $\mathrm{CO}_2$ and $\mathrm{N}_2$,
pose life-threatening risks in confined spaces. Gas sensors can therefore
provide \textbf{early warning before concentration levels reach dangerous
thresholds}. Another major application area is air-quality monitoring: urban
pollution networks track $\mathrm{NO}_x$ and $\mathrm{SO}_2$; indoor-air
sensors measure volatile organic compound (VOC) and $\mathrm{CO}_2$ levels;
and climate monitoring stations record greenhouse gases. Gas sensing is equally
prominent in industrial settings, including $\mathrm{O}_2$ monitoring for
combustion optimisation, trace-gas management in semiconductor cleanrooms, and
process control in chemical reactors. One field that is continuously gaining
importance is agricultural gas sensing.
There are three main types of gas sensing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Catalytic (combustion-based)
  \item Optical---Non-Dispersive Infrared (NDIR)
  \item Electrochemical
\end{itemize}

Today, ideal gas sensing remains an open research problem. Key challenges
include selectivity (the ability to distinguish between chemically similar
gases), sensitivity (detection at sub-\text{ppm} to \text{ppb} concentration
levels), stability (resistance to drift and ageing), and power efficiency---all
of which remain prominent difficulties in current sensor technology.

\gls{mox} sensor technology is currently the most widely used method for electronic
gas sensing. Their operating principle is based on a heated semiconducting
metal-oxide layer whose electrical resistance varies with exposure to oxidizing
or reducing gases. While \gls{mox} sensors are inexpensive and widely used, they
typically require high operating temperatures, humidity compensation, and often
suffer from long-term baseline drift. These limitations motivate the
development of alternative gas-sensing technologies with improved stability,
power consumption, and selectivity. Recent research trends emphasize highly
sensitive, low-power gas sensors, especially those leveraging micro- and
nano-scale structures. The \gls{gmos} is one such candidate. It is based on a
thermal \gls{mox} (\gls{tmos}) device implemented in \gls{cmos}-\gls{soi}
technology. \gls{tmos} is a suspended \gls{mosfet} operating in the
subthreshold region, making it highly temperature-sensitive and
low-power. This technology was adapted to create the GMOS gas sensor—a
combustion-based device that uses a catalytic layer and an integrated tungsten
heater. When heated and exposed to specific gases, catalytic reactions alter
the local temperature, and this thermal change is measured through the
subthreshold \gls{mosfet}. The GMOS device benefits from the
\gls{cmos}-\gls{soi}-\gls{mems} process, which provides strong thermal
isolation and allows a large-area \gls{mosfet} structure with enhanced
responsivity. \\
It is evident that R\&D activity in gas sensing is extensive. The present study
focuses on GMOS-based detection of ethylene at low concentrations, motivated by
its critical importance in agriculture and food safety.

\subsection{How to Improve GMOS Gas Sensor Performance}
\subsubsection{GMOS theoretical background}
The GMOS transistor detects gas via an exothermic combustion process. We 
transform transistor into a gas sensing device by mechanically separating it 
from its surrounding substrate using a micro-machining process, thus creating 
a device that is thermally isolated. An additional catalytic layer is added on 
top of the device. Catalysts are substances that speed up the reaction but are 
not consumed by it and do not appear in the net reaction equation. An active
pixel is one which has a layer of catalyst on the surface of the device,
allowing the chemical reaction to occur at a temperature lower than
the usual ignition threshold denoted by $T^*$ for the specific gas being sampled. 
When a pixel is heated to a nominal temperature high enough to cause the
exothermic chemical reaction with the catalyst, tiny explosions occur near the
surface of the GMOS sensor, causing a slight shift in $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ and
changing the transistor's drain
current. To archive the ignition temperature, the catalytic layers temperature 
is first increased by Joule heating dissipated by a resistor in contact with the 
reaction plate. The value of the required resistor is determined by the following 
heat flow equation:

\begin{equation}
  C_\mathrm{th} \frac{d \left T-T_0 \right}{dt} + G_\mathrm{th} \left T-T_0 \right = P_\mathrm{Joule-heating} + P_\mathrm{reaction}
\end{equation}

Where at steady state,

\begin{equation}
  T = T_0 + \Delta T_J + + \Delta T_R = T_0 + \frac{P_\mathrm{Joule-heating}}{G_\mathrm{th}} + \frac{P_\mathrm{reaction}}{G_\mathrm{th}}
\end{equation}

$T_0$ is the frame temperature which is determined by the ambient temperature 
and the additional heat generated by the heating resistor:

\begin{equation}
  P_\mathrm{Joule-heating} = I^2 R(T) = \frac{V^2}{R(T)}
\end{equation}

$P_\mathrm{reaction}$ is the power released by the exothermic oxidation reaction 

\begin{equation}
  \Delta T_\mathrm{reaction} = \frac{P_\mathrm{reaction}}{G_\mathrm{th}}
\end{equation}

At steady state and in an absence of a combustive reaction, the Joule heating 
increases the GMOS temperature according to the following relation:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta T_J = \frac{I \cdot V}{G_\mathrm{th}} = \frac{I^2 \cdot R(T)}{G_\mathrm{th}} = \frac{V_2}{R(T) \cdot G_\mathrm{th}}
\end{equation}

Where $R(T)$ is the resistor heating the reaction plate and $V$ is the applied 
voltage to the heater.

A readout system must therefore be capable of extracting a $\approx 10\,\mu\text{V}$

That work distinguishes two readout topologies: a diode-connected (2T)
configuration and an amplification (3T) configuration, and derives the
fundamental equations governing the electrical behaviour of the GMOS device.
In the present work, the 2T connection is adopted for its simplicity and
measurement reproducibility.

In the 2T configuration, the GMOS drain is shorted to its gate, enforcing
$V_{\mathrm{DS}} = V_{\mathrm{GS}} = V$. In the subthreshold regime the
drain current follows:

\begin{equation}
  I_{D} = I_0 \exp\left(\frac{V}{nV_T}\right)
  \label{eq:subthreshold_current}
\end{equation}

where $V_T = k_BT/q$ is the thermal voltage, $n$ is the slope factor
(typically $1.5$--$2.5$), and $I_0$ is a process-dependent pre-exponential
constant.

The pre-exponential factor $I_0$ depends on temperature as:

\begin{equation}
  I_0(T) = \mu(T)\,C_{\mathrm{ox}}\frac{W}{L}(n-1)\left(\frac{k_BT}{q}\right)^2
  \label{eq:I0_temperature}
\end{equation}

A gas-induced exothermic reaction raises the local temperature of the active
pixel, shifting $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ and producing a measurable change in drain
current proportional to the gas concentration. The fractional current change
is related to the temperature perturbation by $\Delta I/I = \alpha_T\,\Delta T$,
where the thermal sensitivity coefficient is:

\begin{equation}
  \alpha_T = \frac{\partial \ln I}{\partial T} \approx \frac{E_a}{k_B T^2}
  \label{eq:alpha_T}
\end{equation}

The amplitude of the drain-current modulation at angular frequency $\omega$,
produced by a periodic thermal stimulus of amplitude $\Delta T$, is:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta I(\omega) = I_0\,\alpha_T\,\frac{K_H C}{G_{\mathrm{th}}(1-j\omega\tau)}
  \label{eq:delta_I}
\end{equation}

yields the normalised thermal sensitivity:

\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{I_D}\frac{\mathrm{d}I_D}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{V}{nV_T^2}\frac{k_B}{q}

\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Dynamic Operation Instead of Static Sensing}
Accurate readout of the transistor response is challenged by low-frequency
noise sources, particularly flicker noise, which hinders the signal-to-noise
ratio for measurements in \gls{dc} mode. To address this, the present work
investigates the incorporation of \gls{lia} techniques into the GMOS readout
path. Lock-in amplifiers extract weak periodic signals from noise by performing
synchronous detection at a known reference frequency. By shifting the
measurement away from the low-frequency noise spectrum and using narrow-band
filtering, \glspl{lia} can significantly improve signal-to-noise ratio and
enable more reliable extraction of temperature-induced variations in the GMOS
transistor. This thesis focuses on developing and demonstrating a lock-in-based
readout method for the GMOS sensor, aiming to improve measurement stability,
noise performance, and overall device usability.  Implementation of an
environment sensing system based on the GMOS gas sensor has been explored in
previous work, showing promising results. However, new experimental results
that shed new light on the noise properties of the sensor have introduced new
prospects of improvement. \\
One key area for improvement is the implementation of the readout circuit. So
far, measurements have been focused on differential measurements of the
operation points of two NMOS transistors in the sub-threshold region. The
operational point varies with temperature due to exposure to gas; one
transistor has an active catalyst while the other has no catalytic layer.
Subtracting the voltage drop of each transistor and amplifying the result
yields a readout proportional to the gas concentration. As it turns out, the
dominant noise in this kind of measurement method is the flicker noise.
Previous results and experiments with the GMOS device have shown that the
device is capable of sensing gas concentrations in the range of
$10$--$50\,\text{ppm}$. While theoretical calculations have previously shown
that the current sensor can theoretically detect concentrations as low as
$0.5\,\text{ppm}$.

\subsection{Lock-In (LIA) Operation Principles}
\gls{cmos} technology is the most prominent technology in microelectronics used for
various fields and applications such as analog circuits, digital logic, RF and
sensing circuits. With the successful trend of miniaturization of integrated
circuits, IC designers managed to increase operating frequency and decrease
power consumption. While successful in these stated fields, the decreased device
size comes at a cost of increased flicker noise. Low-frequency, high-amplitude
noise limits the performance and functionality of analog and digital circuits,
indirectly increasing the noise figure of amplifiers and voltage converters. Such
noise degrades the \gls{snr} of \gls{cmos}-based sensors such as \gls{ir} and
image sensors.
Flicker noise, also widely known as $1/f$ noise, has a \gls{psd} proportional to
$f^{-\gamma (f)}$ where $0.8 \leq \gamma (f) \leq 1.2$. Flicker noise tends to
be the dominant noise factor in low-frequency measurements, where it is
significantly higher than the background thermal white noise component. The
physical source of flicker noise has been the subject of extensive research.
Measurements conducted on semiconductors indicated that there is a relation between
surface state and noise spectrum, while measurements of conducting materials
exhibited an inverse relation between the noise spectrum and material volume.
Lock-in amplifiers are used to detect and measure low-amplitude \gls{ac} signals even
in the presence of strong background noise. The technique is based on
phase-sensitive measurement, which enables detecting the frequency component of
the signal and its relative phase while rejecting other noisy frequencies. The
lock-in approach typically requires three elements: a waveform generator, a
mixer, and a low-pass filter. The waveform generator produces a periodic signal
of frequency $f_{\mathrm{ref}}$ that modulates the target signal and also serves as an
internal reference signal.

\begin{equation}
  V_{\mathrm{ref}} = V \sin(2 \pi f_{\mathrm{ref}} t + \phi_{\mathrm{ref}} )
  \label{eq:ref_signal}
\end{equation}

The mixer multiplies the target signal and the reference signal. The resulting
signal is fed into a low-pass filter that rejects any non-\gls{dc} components of the
mixed signal, leaving a slow-varying \gls{dc} signal that is proportional to the
instantaneous amplitude of the target signal. In the following proposed
architecture of a lock-in amplifier mentioned in \cite{lowpower_lia_placeholder}

A current reference frequency is fed through an unknown impedance translated to
a voltage through an op-amp based transconductance amplifier. The signal is
then passed through a \gls{bpf} (which is optional and is mainly used to reject
any frequencies that are higher or lower than the reference signal's
frequency). The resulting signal is fed into two mixers with the same reference
frequency (with $\pi / 2$ phase difference). Then the resulting signal is
filtered through a \gls{lpf} to obtain the \gls{dc} component. We can analyze
the result by denoting the mixer output signals as: 

\begin{align}
  I(t) &= V_\mathrm{sig}\sin(2\pi f_\mathrm{sig}t + \phi_\mathrm{sig})
          \cdot\sin(2\pi f_\mathrm{ref}t + \phi_\mathrm{ref})
          \nonumber\\
       &= \frac{V_\mathrm{sig}}{2}\left[
            \cos\left(2\pi(f_\mathrm{sig}-f_\mathrm{ref})t
                      +\phi_\mathrm{sig}-\phi_\mathrm{ref}\right)
          - \cos\left(2\pi(f_\mathrm{sig}+f_\mathrm{ref})t
                      +\phi_\mathrm{sig}+\phi_\mathrm{ref}\right)
          \right]
          \label{eq:I_channel}\\
  Q(t) &= V_\mathrm{sig}\sin(2\pi f_\mathrm{sig}t + \phi_\mathrm{sig})
          \cdot\cos(2\pi f_\mathrm{ref}t + \phi_\mathrm{ref})
          \nonumber\\
       &= \frac{V_\mathrm{sig}}{2}\left[
            \sin\left(2\pi(f_\mathrm{sig}+f_\mathrm{ref})t
                      +\phi_\mathrm{sig}+\phi_\mathrm{ref}\right)
          + \sin\left(2\pi(f_\mathrm{sig}-f_\mathrm{ref})t
                      +\phi_\mathrm{sig}-\phi_\mathrm{ref}\right)
          \right]
          \label{eq:Q_channel}
\end{align}
When $f_\mathrm{sig} = f_\mathrm{ref}$, the difference-frequency argument
$2\pi(f_\mathrm{sig}-f_\mathrm{ref})t$ vanishes, so the \gls{dc} terms reduce
to constant phase offsets $\cos(\phi_\mathrm{sig}-\phi_\mathrm{ref})$ and
$\sin(\phi_\mathrm{sig}-\phi_\mathrm{ref})$ respectively. The sum-frequency
terms, oscillating at $2f_\mathrm{ref}$, are attenuated by the \gls{lpf},
leaving the \gls{dc} outputs:
\begin{align}
  I_\mathrm{DC} &= \frac{V_\mathrm{sig}}{2}\cos(\phi_\mathrm{sig}-\phi_\mathrm{ref})
                   \label{eq:I_dc}\\
  Q_\mathrm{DC} &= \frac{V_\mathrm{sig}}{2}\sin(\phi_\mathrm{sig}-\phi_\mathrm{ref})
                   \label{eq:Q_dc}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{The Actuators: How to Modulate The Heater Resistor}
Rather than applying a DC bias to the integrated heater resistors, the LIA reference output applies a sinusoidal voltage at frequency $f_{\mathrm{ref}}$:

\begin{equation}
  V_H(t) = V_{\mathrm{ref}}\sin\left(2\pi f_{\mathrm{ref}} t\right)
  \label{eq:heater_voltage}
\end{equation}

The instantaneous power dissipated in each heater is:

\begin{equation}
  P_H(t) = R_H \left(I_{\mathrm{DC}}^2 + 2I_{\mathrm{DC}}\,i\cos(\omega t)
            + i^2\cos^2(\omega t)\right)
  \label{eq:heater_power}
\end{equation}

Where $i$ is the amplitude of the AC modulation current. The $i^2$ term is assumed
negligible. Both heater resistors, $R_{\mathrm{H,blind}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{H,active}}$,
are driven in parallel and are assumed to have equal values and equal temperature
coefficients of resistance (TCR). Imbalances between the two heater resistors will be
addressed separately.

\subsection{The Sensing Thermal Sensor Mechanism}
The GMOS performs thermal-offset subtraction by reading the differential drain
voltage between a blind and an active pixel. A catalyst layer is applied to the
active pixel, while both pixels experience the same ambient temperature, supply
noise, and heater-induced thermal background. Taking their difference therefore
isolates only the additional threshold-voltage shift caused by the gas
reaction:

\begin{align}
  \Delta V_{\mathrm{out}} &= V_A - V_B
                              \label{eq:diff_voltage_def}\\
  \Delta V_{\mathrm{out}} &= \left(I_{\mathrm{D,blind}} - I_{\mathrm{D,active}}\right) R_s
                              \label{eq:diff_voltage}
\end{align}

Where $V_A$ and $V_B$ are the drain voltages of the active and blind pixels,
$R_s$ is the series resistor, and $V_{\mathrm{DD}}$ is the supply voltage.
Sensing in differential mode suppresses the residual common-mode noise and
thermal effects experienced by both transistors, leaving behind only the
voltage change attributable to the catalytic reaction on the active pixel.

The choice of the biasing resistor $R_s$ must satisfy the requirement that
the voltage drop across it remains negligible relative to the transistor output
impedance, formally:

\begin{equation}
  R_s \ll r_{ds} = \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{ds}}}{\partial i_d}
  \label{eq:bias_condition}
\end{equation}

Additionally, when a chemical reaction shifts $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ and hence $I_D$,
the resulting current variation must be small enough that the operating point
does not move significantly. As described by \eqref{eq:subthreshold_current},
a threshold shift $\delta V_{\mathrm{th}}$ produces a fractional current change
$\delta I_D / I_D = \delta V_{\mathrm{th}} / (nV_T)$. Requiring this to remain
small imposes:

\begin{equation}
  R_s \gg \frac{nV_T}{I_D}
  \label{eq:Rs_condition}
\end{equation}

This requirement is typically satisfied with $R_s \geq 500\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ at
typical GMOS subthreshold bias levels. Both blind and active resistors must
match in value so that the differential output defined in \eqref{eq:diff_voltage}
is not corrupted by a resistor mismatch.
